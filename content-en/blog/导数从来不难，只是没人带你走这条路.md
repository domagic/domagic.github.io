# Derivatives: From Average Rate of Change to Instantaneous Rate of Change

Suppose you are driving past a speed camera. The needle on your dashboard points to 80, and you know that is your speed at that instant. But stop and think: speed equals distance divided by time, and the length of time of an "instant" is zero. The distance travelled in zero seconds is zero. Zero divided by zero has no meaning.

So where did that 80 come from?

This question puzzled humanity for over a thousand years. What it really asks is: can a single **instant** have a speed? On the path below, we start from elementary arithmetic and walk step by step to the threshold of the derivative. You will find that every step merely pushes something familiar forward a little.

## 1. Average rate of change

Start with something you could calculate at ten years old.

Suppose a car travels 120 km between 2 o'clock and 4 o'clock. The average speed is distance divided by time:

$$
\frac{120\ \text{km}}{2\ \text{h}} = 60\ \text{km/h}
$$

Put it in mathematical language. Let position be a function of time, $s(t)$. From the moment $t$ to $t+\Delta t$ — here $\Delta t$ denotes a short change in time, read aloud as "delta t" — the position changes from $s(t)$ to $s(t+\Delta t)$. The average speed over this interval is

$$
\bar v = \frac{s(t+\Delta t)-s(t)}{\Delta t} \qquad\mathrm{(1)}
$$

On a graph, join the points $(t, s(t))$ and $(t+\Delta t, s(t+\Delta t))$; this line is called a **secant**, and its slope is exactly the ratio given by formula (1).

There is nothing wrong with average speed; everyone can calculate it. The trouble is that the speed camera cares not about the two-hour average but about the **instant** you pass it.

## 2. From average to instantaneous: the limit

Since an instant is hard to calculate, settle for the next best thing: work out the average speed over a short interval, then make that interval shorter and shorter.

Suppose the position of a freely falling object is $s(t)=\dfrac{1}{2}gt^2$ (where $g$ is the acceleration due to gravity). We want the speed at the instant $t=1$ second. In formula (1), take $s(t)=\dfrac{1}{2}gt^2$ and $t=1$, and first calculate the average speed from 1 second to $1+\Delta t$ seconds:

$$
\frac{s(1+\Delta t)-s(1)}{\Delta t}
= \frac{\frac{1}{2}g(1+\Delta t)^2 - \frac{1}{2}g}{\Delta t}
$$

Expand $(1+\Delta t)^2 = 1 + 2\Delta t + (\Delta t)^2$, substitute, and simplify:

$$
\bar v = \frac{g\Delta t + \frac{1}{2}g(\Delta t)^2}{\Delta t}
= g + \frac{1}{2}g\,\Delta t \qquad\mathrm{(2)}
$$

Notice that in formula (2), $\Delta t$ has vanished from the denominator. Take $g=9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$, so $\dfrac{1}{2}g=4.9$, and formula (2) becomes $\bar v = 9.8+4.9\Delta t$. Take successively smaller values of $\Delta t$ and substitute them one by one:

| $\Delta t$ (s) | Substituting | Average speed $\bar v$ (m/s) |
|---:|---|---:|
| 0.1 | $9.8+4.9\times 0.1 = 9.8+0.49$ | 10.29 |
| 0.01 | $9.8+4.9\times 0.01 = 9.8+0.049$ | 9.849 |
| 0.001 | $9.8+4.9\times 0.001 = 9.8+0.0049$ | 9.8049 |
| 0.0001 | $9.8+4.9\times 0.0001 = 9.8+0.00049$ | 9.80049 |
| 0.00001 | $9.8+4.9\times 0.00001 = 9.8+0.000049$ | 9.800049 |
| 0.000001 | $9.8+4.9\times 0.000001 = 9.8+0.0000049$ | 9.8000049 |

Every time $\Delta t$ shrinks to a tenth of its previous value, the result gains one more digit of agreement with 9.8. The smaller $\Delta t$, the closer the average speed gets to 9.8 — and the value it keeps approaching is precisely $g$.

This process of continually approaching is the **limit**. We never let $\Delta t$ actually equal zero (that would make the denominator meaningless); we only look at where the result heads as $\Delta t$ tends to zero. The answer is $g$. So we say: the instantaneous speed at $t=1$ second is $g$.

An instant, in this way, has been "calculated". The method is not to measure the instant directly but to approach it with ever shorter intervals and see which definite number the average speed tends towards.

## 3. The definition of the derivative

Extract the method of the previous section and you have the derivative.

For a general function $y=f(x)$, let the increment of the independent variable, $\Delta x$, tend to zero. The average rate of change in formula (1) is then written as

$$
\frac{f(x+\Delta x)-f(x)}{\Delta x} \qquad\mathrm{(3)}
$$

If it tends to a definite limit, we say that $f$ is **differentiable** at $x$, and call that limit the **derivative** of $f$ at $x$, written $f'(x)$:

$$
\textcolor{#d9480f}{f'(x) = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{f(x+\Delta x)-f(x)}{\Delta x}} \qquad\mathrm{(4)}
$$

Every part of formula (4) is familiar: the numerator is the change in the function's value, the denominator is the change in the independent variable, their ratio is the average rate of change of formula (3), and the $\lim$ in front tells us to let the denominator tend to zero. The only new thing is writing "getting closer and closer" in rigorous notation. This rigorous formulation of derivatives and limits was not established until the nineteenth century, by Cauchy (Cauchy, 1821), while the germ of the derivative concept goes back to Newton's method of fluxions and Leibniz's differential calculus (Newton, 1687; Leibniz, 1684).

As $x$ changes, $f'(x)$ generally changes too, so it is itself a function, called the **derived function**. The derivative $f'(x)$ describes how fast the function is changing at the point $x$.

## 4. Geometric meaning: the tangent line

The geometric picture of the derivative makes this definition visible.

![A secant gradually approaching the tangent](assets/derivative-instantaneous-rate/figure-01.png)

**Figure 1  As point Q slides along the curve towards point P, the secant rotates about P and tends to the tangent; the slope of the tangent is the derivative**

In Figure 1, fix a point P on the curve, take another point Q, and join them with a secant. The slope of the secant is the average rate of change over the interval. Now let Q slide along the curve towards P: as $\Delta x$ gets smaller, the secant hugs the curve's direction near P ever more closely. When Q approaches P without limit, the secant tends to a limiting position — this is the **tangent line** to the curve at P.

The derivative thus acquires a second identity: it is both the instantaneous rate of change and the slope of the tangent line. Algebraic "speed of change" and geometric "steepness" are the same number here. This is half of the derivative's power — it ties an abstract concept to a drawable picture.

## 5. Computing a derivative: an example

A definition is worthless if you can only look at it and never use it. Let us actually compute something with it.

Let $f(x)=x^2$ and find its derivative at an arbitrary $x$. Following the definition in formula (4), first write the difference quotient:

$$
\frac{f(x+\Delta x)-f(x)}{\Delta x}
= \frac{(x+\Delta x)^2 - x^2}{\Delta x}
$$

Expand the numerator: $(x+\Delta x)^2 = x^2 + 2x\Delta x + (\Delta x)^2$; subtracting $x^2$ leaves just $2x\Delta x + (\Delta x)^2$. Substitute back and cancel $\Delta x$:

$$
= \frac{2x\Delta x + (\Delta x)^2}{\Delta x}
= 2x + \Delta x
$$

Finally let $\Delta x \to 0$:

$$
f'(x) = (x^2)' = 2x \qquad\mathrm{(5)}
$$

The result $2x$ is a function: the slope is 2 at $x=1$ and 6 at $x=3$. The parabola $y=x^2$ gets steeper as you move right, and $2x$ faithfully records this.

The same method yields the derivatives of a whole set of basic functions: $(c)'=0$, $(x)'=1$, $(\sin x)'=\cos x$, $(\mathrm{e}^x)'=\mathrm{e}^x$. There is no need to memorise them; derive them once from the definition whenever you need them.

## 6. Applications of the derivative

The real value of the derivative is that it provides a universal language for describing "change".

In physics, differentiating position with respect to time gives velocity, and differentiating velocity again gives acceleration. The answer to the speed-camera question at the start is the derivative: what the dashboard displays is the derivative of the position function at a particular moment.

In economics, differentiating cost with respect to output gives the **marginal cost** — how much cost rises when one more unit is produced. Decision-making is often concerned with "what happens if we do a bit more", which is exactly what the derivative answers.

The derivative can also find extrema. Look at Figure 2: where the function $f(x)=x^3-3x$ is rising, the derivative $f'(x)>0$; where it is falling, $f'(x)<0$. So at the turning point from rising to falling — a local maximum — the derivative is exactly zero; at the turning point from falling to rising — a local minimum — the derivative is also zero.

![The correspondence between a function and its derivative](assets/derivative-instantaneous-rate/figure-02.png)

**Figure 2  Where $f$ is increasing $f'>0$, where it is decreasing $f'<0$; zeros of the derivative correspond to extrema**

Finding the maximum or minimum of a quantity — profit, output, efficiency — thus becomes a matter of solving $f'(x)=0$ and then examining how the derivative given by formula (4) changes sign on either side of the zero. Before the derivative, extremum problems had to be attacked one by one with ingenious tricks; with the derivative, they become a procedure you can follow.

## 7. Summary

Now return to the 80 at the beginning.

It is a limit: squeeze the measurement time down towards zero and see which number the average speed approaches. The camera may not literally use limits to compute, but the reading it displays expresses the same thing as the derivative — the rate of change at an instant.

From average to instantaneous rate of change, from secant to tangent, from a specific function to a general method — this is the whole process by which the concept of the derivative was built. Each step merely pushes something familiar forward: compress the average over a longer interval in formula (1) into a single instant; bring the two endpoints of the secant together into one point; turn the indeterminate form $\frac{0}{0}$ into the limit defined by formula (4).

The value of the derivative lies precisely in this: using the rigorous language of limits, it answers the seemingly contradictory question "can an instant have a rate of change?".
