Two parallel systematic literature reviews compare 42 livestream shopping studies with 29 TV home shopping studies, organizing theories, antecedents, mediators, moderators and consequences, and characterizing communication structures through a sender-media-receiver framework. The two forms share video demonstration and direct selling, but livestream shopping embeds synchronous, mutually visible interaction into shopping and brings the relationship between the streamer and co-viewers into the explanatory frame. ## 1. Research phenomenon: Two viewers watching the same garment demo online — what can each of them actually do?

Imagine two viewers at home watching a demonstration of a knit sweater. Both can see its colour, cut and how it looks on a model, and both can place an order after watching. The first viewer is watching a TV home shopping programme. The presenter mentions several sizes, but she wants to know exactly how tight the cuffs are. The programme may happen to cover this shortly, or she may never get an answer. If the programme takes calls or text messages, she can send her question in, but whether it is picked up in the following demonstration depends on the presenter and the production team.

The second viewer is watching a shopping livestream with the same question. She asks in the comments for the streamer to hold the cuff closer and try rolling it up. If the streamer sees the message, they can show the detail on the spot. Other viewers in the same session can see the question too, and someone who has just bought the same item may add how it fits them. Such an exchange does not guarantee that every question is answered, but it gives viewers an opportunity: to bring the detail they care about into the ongoing demonstration, and to turn other people's questions and experiences into shopping clues of their own.

These two scenes illustrate how TV Home Shopping (TVHS) and Livestream Shopping (LSS) are related. Both bring product demonstrations into consumers' homes through moving images and commentary. In a TV shopping programme, the presentation is usually organized and broadcast by the programme side. In a livestream, the presentation can also be prepared in advance, but live questions and answers have a chance to change what is demonstrated next and what information is added. Both channels let people see products at a distance; what the paper asks is **who can take part in the presentation, whose feedback becomes visible to others, and whether these changes alter the process by which consumers form judgements.**

## 2. Research question: How do we judge whether livestream shopping is conceptually new?

If livestream shopping is defined by "having a presenter, having video, and being able to order from home," it is close to TV home shopping. When evaluating the two channels, consumers may also care about the same things — whether the information is sufficient, whether buying is convenient, and whether the price is right. Yet while watching the same product, viewers sometimes base their decision on materials supplied by the programme, and sometimes form judgements from the streamer's responses to questions, other viewers' experiences and continued interaction. The research distinction has to be understood from how these judgements are produced.

The authors therefore return to existing research models, looking at what each body of literature treats as the starting point of consumer response and what it uses to explain why consumers buy, keep watching or are willing to share with others. Study one organizes 42 livestream shopping papers and study two organizes 29 TV home shopping papers. Both sets undergo the same comparison and are finally placed in an integrated framework. The evidence this yields comes from **how the two bodies of existing research define the channels, select theories and explain behaviour**. The paper's output is a comparison of concepts and their mechanisms; it did not arrange for the same group of consumers to complete shopping tasks in the two types of channel.

## 3. Scientific questions and the logic of comparison

**Table 2. Three levels of conceptual comparison**

| Level of comparison | Specific question | What to look for in the literature |
|---|---|---|
| Communication structure | Who presents the product, in what ways can viewers respond, and can others see those responses? | Sender, medium, receiver and the flow of information |
| Behaviour formation | Why do viewers trust product information or choose to keep participating? Under what conditions does this relationship change? | Theories, antecedents, mediators and moderators |
| Outcomes | Do studies care about how long viewers watch, whether they buy, or whether they have formed a connection with the streamer or other viewers? | Consequences such as purchase, viewing, satisfaction, engagement and sharing |

In the cuff example above, "viewers can ask questions in real time" belongs to communication structure. Whether the streamer gives an effective answer to that question can become an antecedent explaining consumer response. Consumers trusting the streamer's advice more, or feeling more certain that the garment suits their needs, are two possible mediating processes. Staying in the livestream to keep watching, following the streamer, or placing an order are different outcomes. Unfolding these levels one by one reveals that different behavioural processes may underlie the same purchase.

This also shows that comparing the two bodies of literature cannot stop at whether they use the same word. "Trust" appears in both, but trusting the presenter, trusting the product and trusting the transaction channel refer to three different judgements. The authors attend at once to the variable names used in the studies, the objects those variables refer to, and the role they play in the explanatory chain.

## 4. Methods and integrated framework at a glance

### 4.1 How do the two bodies of research enter the same comparison framework?

![Figure 2. Systematic literature review procedure](assets/2024-JBR-Is-livestream-shopping-conceptually-new/figure-02.png)

**Figure 2. Overview of our systematic review procedure.**

The authors used a Systematic Literature Review, carried out through the three stages of assembling, arranging and assessing set out in SPAR-4-SLR. Figure 2 reads from top to bottom. At the top, the target literature is first defined: English-language, peer-reviewed papers published or forthcoming between 1990 and July 2022 that study consumer behaviour in the two shopping formats. The journal scope follows the CABS or ABDC listings. Initial searches of Scopus and Web of Science yielded 231 papers after deduplication; a supplementary Google Scholar search added 165 more, so 396 entered review.

The middle of the figure shows how the set was narrowed from 396 to 71. The team excluded 324 off-topic papers — for example, studies of game livestreaming that did not examine product sales — and one further review to avoid double-counting the research variables it relayed. The screening keeps the comparison object within the two **shopping** contexts. Watching television programmes in general and participating in ordinary livestreams, even when they involve a presenter or an audience, belong to different questions.

After inclusion, the authors recorded the theories, antecedents, mediators, moderators and consequences adopted by each paper using a consistent scheme, along with the year, method, market and platform. They then used Content Analysis to compare how the two groups explain behaviour. One paper may study "streamer response speed" and "consumer trust," while another studies "information transmission quality" and "consumer trust." Consistent organization preserves the concepts they share while also preserving the difference that one starts from interpersonal responses and the other from medium functionality.

### 4.2 How should the integrated framework of sender, medium and receiver be read?

![Figure 3. Integrated framework of the livestream shopping and TV home shopping literatures](assets/2024-JBR-Is-livestream-shopping-conceptually-new/figure-03.png)

**Figure 3. An integrative framework: Comparison of LSS and TVHS literatures.**

Both halves of Figure 3 describe the shopping process through three positions: Sender, Media and Receiver. In the upper half, double-headed arrows show that information can flow both ways. The streamer presents the product, viewers can actively ask questions and make requests, and can draw on the feedback of co-viewers. The research covers dedicated livestreaming platforms as well as the livestream features built into e-commerce sites and social platforms. Because these media allow different forms of participation, the authors summarize livestream shopping on the right as a Marketing Tool for Engagement. Selling goods remains the goal, but maintaining a relationship with viewers is also an outcome researchers care about.

In the lower half, the arrows run mainly from the presenter to the viewers. Television programmes provide demonstrations, concentrated product explanations and shopping convenience for people who cannot easily go out. Some programmes accept calls or text messages from viewers, but whether an individual piece of feedback enters the programme still depends on the programme side's choice. Viewers mainly receive the programme content. The right side therefore highlights the use of TV home shopping as a Direct Selling Channel: the programme organizes and conveys product information so that consumers can complete a purchase remotely.

Moving from the left of the figure to the right shows how the authors connect two judgements. They first compare communication structure, then examine what existing research actually attributes behaviour to. The double- and single-headed arrows depict information flows; the theories and variables listed alongside are the material the authors use to judge where the research emphasis lies.

## 5. Comparison results

### 5.1 The two formats share a common shopping foundation

Consider first where the two bodies of literature overlap. Consumers may choose either format because they can see a product demonstration at home, obtain useful information and order conveniently. Researchers therefore examine Utilitarian Value, Convenience or Hedonic Value in both, and observe purchase intention, satisfaction and loyalty in both. Both groups have used the Elaboration Likelihood Model and Media Richness Theory. TV home shopping serves as a valid reference for livestream shopping precisely because these basic conditions exist in both channels.

### 5.2 Where behaviour starts: the streamer in one, channel functionality in the other

![Figure 4. Distribution of antecedents in the livestream shopping and TV home shopping literatures](assets/2024-JBR-Is-livestream-shopping-conceptually-new/figure-04.png)

**Figure 4. Antecedents in the LSS versus TVHS literatures.**

The bars on the left of Figure 4 correspond to the livestream shopping literature. Of 126 antecedent codes, 68 concern the streamer, or 54%. Some studies explain why viewers are willing to keep interacting through the streamer's expertise, likeability and response quality. Another nine concern co-viewers — for example, whether other people's feedback or mood changes one's own view of the livestream and the product. This latter category appears in the included livestream shopping literature but has no counterpart coded in the TV home shopping group.

The right side corresponds to the TV home shopping literature. Of 50 antecedent codes, the largest category concerns the channel, with 19 codes or 38%. Typical questions are whether the programme demonstrates the product adequately, whether consumers find shopping by television convenient, and whether the payment process runs smoothly. The contrast between the two bars shows where researchers habitually begin: in explaining livestream shopping, the streamer and co-viewers often become the starting point of behaviour; in explaining TV shopping, the starting point is more often how the medium provides information and completes the shopping task.

### 5.3 How behaviour forms: the respective roles of streamer relationships and channel evaluations

![Figure 5. Distribution of mediators in the livestream shopping and TV home shopping literatures](assets/2024-JBR-Is-livestream-shopping-conceptually-new/figure-05.png)

**Figure 5. Mediators in the LSS versus TVHS literatures.**

Seeing a streamer's reply does not mean a consumer immediately decides to buy. What may happen in between is that the reply makes the viewer feel the streamer understands their needs, and so makes them more willing to trust the streamer. It may also create mutual understanding in a short interaction — what some studies call swift guanxi. The livestream shopping literature also discusses parasocial interaction, the subjective experience of interacting with a media figure. This concept can enter a model alongside actual replies in chat, describing respectively the relationship the viewer feels and the exchange that actually happens. Of 75 mediator codes in livestream shopping, 29 are streamer-related, or 39%.

The TV home shopping example is this: a consumer who finds it difficult to shop in person can see a product demonstration through the programme and therefore find the channel convenient. That sense of convenience in turn explains why the consumer is more satisfied with TV shopping. Another line of explanation involves trust in the shopping channel or perceived risk. Of 25 mediator codes, seven are channel-related, or 28%, the largest category in that group. The remaining three categories each account for 24%, a fairly even distribution. So when the authors characterize TV shopping as channel-focused, they also draw on the theoretical and antecedent evidence.

### 5.4 Which conditions change the relationships, and what do studies use to measure outcomes?

Moderators in livestream shopping research often concern the viewing setting. For example, when a livestream room is crowded, or when a viewer is familiar with it, does the same degree of interaction more readily produce a close bond? TV home shopping research more often asks about consumer characteristics — whether people of different cultural backgrounds, viewing experience or levels of loneliness adopt the channel in different ways. Both bodies of literature study purchase, satisfaction and loyalty. Livestream shopping research additionally includes sharing, continued engagement and willingness to give gifts among its outcomes, using these to observe how the streamer–viewer relationship continues beyond the transaction.

**Table 3. Main comparison results of the two bodies of literature**

| Dimension | Livestream shopping literature | TV home shopping literature |
|---|---|---|
| Theoretical orientation | Mixed Orientation is the largest coded category; social exchange, attachment and parasocial interaction are also used to explain relational mechanisms | Studies that use theory more often take a Media Orientation; of the 29 studies, 19 do not explicitly use a theory |
| Common antecedents | Streamer expertise, likeability and interaction style; co-viewers are also studied | Channel information richness, convenience and payment experience |
| Common mediators | Streamer trust, swift guanxi, parasocial interaction | Channel convenience, reliance on shopping, channel trust, perceived risk |
| Moderator examples | Platform type, room popularity, viewing duration, membership tenure | Culture, gender, usage experience, loneliness |
| Outcome emphasis | Purchase and satisfaction, along with engagement, sharing, stickiness and willingness to give gifts | Purchase and satisfaction, along with programme viewing and purchase frequency |

The authors argue that livestream shopping is conceptually new on two grounds. First, synchronous communication and public feedback have changed who can participate in generating product information. Second, the theories and variables in the two bodies of literature reflect different explanatory emphases: livestream shopping emphasizes how viewers form relationships with the streamer and other viewers, while TV home shopping emphasizes how the medium helps consumers obtain information and complete a purchase. The conceptual distinction rests on these processes.

## 6. Advanced analysis

### 6.1 How should literature frequencies be read without mistaking them for consumer effects?

The 54% in Figure 4 can easily be misread as "more than half of livestream shopping papers study the streamer." The actual denominator is 126 antecedent codes, not 42 papers. A paper that examines streamer expertise, likeability and synchronicity at once contributes codes to both the streamer and channel categories. The proportion describes the distribution of variables across the included literature. To answer how many papers studied the streamer, one would have to recount using the number of papers that use at least one streamer variable.

Theoretical proportions need the same reading. The 42 livestream shopping papers used 29 different theories, with the Stimulus–Organism–Response (S–O–R) framework used seven times. Mixed Orientation accounts for 44.2% of coded theory applications and Social Orientation for 21.2%. A mixed framework can study the medium, the streamer and the consumer at once, and can embed interpersonal relationships in its explanatory chain. So "Mixed Orientation is the largest theoretical category" and "livestream shopping research emphasizes social and relational processes" can both hold at the same time. The first describes the frameworks adopted; the second describes what those frameworks explain.

Frequencies and effect sizes answer different questions. Sixty-eight streamer-related antecedents mean that studies often choose such variables. To compare whether streamer responsiveness or payment convenience has a greater effect on actual purchasing, one would need comparable outcomes and effect estimates. This paper shows, by organizing the literature, why each shopping format has mechanisms worth studying.

### 6.2 How should the same concept be placed back into its original model?

Take trust as an example. In one explanatory chain in livestream shopping, viewers feel the streamer patiently answered a question about sizing and therefore trust the advice the streamer gives more, and that trust is associated with purchase intention. Here the antecedent is Interaction Quality, the mediator is trust in the streamer, and the outcome is Purchase Intention. If we then ask "does room popularity change the link between interaction and relationship strength?", we have moved to a moderation question — whether the same relationship is stronger or weaker under different conditions.

Switch the example to TV shopping: studies ask whether the programme's information is rich, whether payment is convenient, and how much consumers therefore trust the channel. Even if both studies use trust as a mediator, giving the streamer more chances to reply and simplifying the payment process change different links. Confirming who a concept refers to, then asking which step it explains in the model, allows older theories to be applied more precisely to the new livestream setting.

### 6.3 Under what conditions does immediate interaction give consumers more control?

From the sender–medium–receiver framework, livestreaming offers a Technological Affordance: consumers have an opportunity to ask questions, and others have an opportunity to see or supplement the answers. Consumer Empowerment requires looking further at whether consumers actually gain more say and control over information from this process. In the cuff demonstration, being able to post the comment is one layer; the streamer seeing and replying is another; whether the answer helps the consumer make a judgement still has to be established.

Making this distinction lets a study measure each link separately — for example, recording whether viewers asked questions, whether those questions were answered, and whether those who received replies found the information they needed more easily. Only then is it possible to test whether this sense of control connects to engagement or purchase. This path is also an example of how the paper moves from conceptual comparison toward future research.

### 6.4 What questions for further testing does the review propose?

**Table 4. Livestream shopping research directions proposed by the authors**

| Direction | Questions that can be studied further |
|---|---|
| Segment livestream shopping mechanisms | Compare platform type, streamer identity, product category and content format. Does the same interaction still foster engagement or purchase in different settings? |
| Validate consumer empowerment | Observe the information and sense of control consumers gain, and test whether they explain the relationship between interactive features and engagement or purchase |
| Combine actual sales data and new methods | Align viewing, interaction and orders in time; image or video analysis can also be used to identify presentation features related to engagement |
| Conduct cross-cultural research | Test whether the same streamer communication style and feedback arrangements elicit similar responses across languages and market settings |
| Study regulation and consumer protection | Examine how commercial disclosure and governance measures affect consumers' recognition of promotional information, formation of trust and purchase judgements |

These directions can be understood by returning to the scenario at the start of the review. Rather than asking only whether live streaming can sell a few more sweaters, researchers can ask: who raised the sizing question the viewer cared about, who answered it, whether others could see the answer, and whether the reply improved subsequent product judgement. Only by making the questions progressively concrete can the communication and relationship mechanisms described in the paper be tested.
